Privacy

Liquid Control privacy policy

Effective November 11, 2026. Manan Bhardwaj ("we") makes Liquid Control: the app for iPhone, the app for Apple Watch, Liquid Control for Mac, and the Liquid Control Stick. Its earlier name was WristPad; nothing about your data changed with the name.

Short version: Liquid Control collects nothing by itself. There's no account, no analytics, no advertising and no tracking, and the apps send nothing to us by themselves. What you do on your watch or iPhone goes to a device you chose: your Mac, a Liquid Control Stick, a computer or TV paired with your iPhone as a Bluetooth keyboard, or a Roku, Google TV, Android TV or LG TV on your Wi-Fi. What you set up on one iPhone reaches your other iPhones and iPads through your own iCloud Keychain, end-to-end encrypted, never through us (see "Your other iPhones and iPads"). Nothing of yours reaches us unless you choose to send it: a link log you email us (see "Send Link Log"), an email, TestFlight feedback, or crash reports you let Apple share with developers.

Pairing

  • Each watch, Mac and Stick has its own pairing key, kept in its Keychain; the watch's never leaves it, not even in a backup. Your iPhones and iPads share one pairing key through your iCloud Keychain (end-to-end encrypted, under your Apple ID), so a Mac or Stick paired from one of them knows the others too.
  • Liquid Control for Mac keeps the public keys of the watches and iPhones paired with it, and an iPhone that passes watches' input on keeps theirs. The watch and the iPhone keep the public key, the kind (Mac, Stick, iPhone), the name and the time last seen of each device they're paired with. Your Mac tells paired remotes its computer name, over the encrypted link.
  • Where text will go (Mac, on by default). While your iPhone is connected, Liquid Control for Mac tells it the name of the app in front and the kind of field that has the cursor (for example "Safari · Address bar"), over the encrypted link, so the iPhone can show where your words will land. Never what's in the field. Turn it off in Liquid Control for Mac's Settings ("Tell my iPhone where text will go"). Your watch isn't sent it.
  • Your devices on your watch. Your iPhone sends your watch its list of devices (each one's name, kind and whether it's connected) over Apple's own watch-to-iPhone connection, so the watch can show and switch them.
  • Your watch and your iPhone exchange their public keys over Apple's own watch-to-iPhone connection, so the watch can work through the iPhone without a code. The iPhone keeps the watch's key until you unpair the watch or delete Liquid Control.
  • Nearby Bluetooth devices can see that Liquid Control is there: your Mac while Liquid Control for Mac runs, and your iPhone while it's passing a watch's input on, while Add a device is waiting for a Bluetooth device, and while a Bluetooth device you added needs it (see "Bluetooth devices"). Without pairing they can't do anything with it.
  • Forget all (Mac Settings), Forget this Mac and Devices → Forget (watch and iPhone), and Forget other watches (iPhone, Watch relay) remove pairings.

Your other iPhones and iPads

What you set up on one iPhone or iPad syncs to your others through your iCloud Keychain: one small item per device, end-to-end encrypted by Apple, under your Apple ID. Liquid Control has no server and no account, and we can't read it. With iCloud Keychain off, nothing syncs.

  • What syncs: your devices (each one's name, kind, place in your list and Stay connected; a Mac's or Stick's public key and the name it said; what a Bluetooth device runs; your Roku's address, serial number, name and model; a Google TV's or an LG TV's address and the fingerprint of its key, and the key an LG TV gave), your settings (pointer, scrolling, voice, Disconnect when idle, each device's surface), your code words and their secrets (see "Code words"), and your own shortcuts.
  • What stays on each iPhone: the device in use, which Bluetooth device is which (each iPhone pairs a Bluetooth device itself), the watch's settings, the websites you opened, hints you've seen, and the logs.
  • Forget on one iPhone forgets on all of them.

Your voice

Recording. Liquid Control records only while you hold a 🎤 button or key, after you start hands-free, or during a test dictation from the Mac's testing tools, and on the Mac for at most 3 minutes at a time. The recording is kept in memory until the words are typed, then dropped. It is never saved to a file, with one exception: the Whisper test below, which you turn on yourself.

On your Mac (Liquid Control for Mac).

  • The Mac records with its own microphone (or AirPods connected to it), or receives the watch's microphone over the encrypted link when the watch is connected straight to the Mac and "Watch mic" is on (off by default). Your iPhone never sends the Mac audio: it turns your words into text itself (below).
  • Liquid Control for Mac turns speech into text with Apple's speech recognition. On macOS 26 and later this runs on your Mac (the speech model downloads from Apple the first time). On macOS 14 and 15 it runs on your Mac when the Mac can recognise your language there; otherwise Apple's speech recognition does it on Apple's servers, under Apple's privacy policy.
  • If everything you said is a voice command (for example "press enter"), the Mac presses that key instead of typing.
  • The text is pasted where your cursor is (see "Text" for how), and your watch or iPhone shows it for a moment.

On your iPhone (for every device, your Mac included, and the watch through the iPhone).

  • The words are recognised on the iPhone itself, with Apple's on-device speech recognition. Liquid Control never uses a server for this: if the iPhone can't recognise your language by itself, Liquid Control says so instead.
  • On iOS 26 and later, the speech model downloads from Apple when you tap to get it.
  • Through the iPhone, the watch's microphone streams over the encrypted link and the iPhone recognises it.
  • What leaves the iPhone is the typed text: key presses to the device you chose. The words show on screen while you talk, and neither the audio nor the text is kept. When you talk through the watch, the words, and a code word's label (never what it types), also go back to the watch over the encrypted link so it can show them.

Whisper mode (iPhone). When it's on, Liquid Control records with as little of the system's voice processing as it can, so whispers aren't filtered out, and uses your AirPods' microphone when they're connected. The sound is made louder before it's recognised. Nothing more is kept; the setting is all that's saved.

The Whisper test (iPhone, Settings → Advanced). An optional test of how well whispering works.

  • It records only after you turn on "Record my voice for this test" on its screen, and only while you hold record: up to 30 seconds per sentence. Leaving the screen turns the test off.
  • Each recording is saved as an audio file in Liquid Control's own folder on this iPhone. The folder is excluded from backups and can't be read while the iPhone is locked.
  • With the recordings, Liquid Control keeps a list: your initials, which sentence each one was for (the test's sentences, and up to 10 of your code words' phrases), the setup, how long it was, when it was made, and the kind of microphone (never its name).
  • Analyse runs on the iPhone and keeps only numbers (and an error message if a run failed), never what was heard.
  • The summary holds the results and your initials only: no audio, no sentences, no transcripts. It leaves the iPhone only if you share it yourself.
  • The recordings stay until you delete them: Delete all takes deletes every recording, the list and the summary at once.

Optional servers on your Mac. Liquid Control for Mac has two settings, off by default and under Settings → Advanced, that send data to a server you set up:

  • a Whisper-compatible transcription server: it receives each recording, your vocabulary hints and the model name;
  • an LLM server that tidies up transcripts: it receives the text, your vocabulary hints and Liquid Control's instructions.

Those servers are run by you or by a provider you choose, under their terms. We never see what's sent. If a server's address starts with http://, what's sent to it isn't encrypted. The API keys you enter for them are kept in Liquid Control's settings on your Mac.

Text, passwords and code words

Text.

  • What you send from Type goes to the device you chose, and isn't kept: the field empties when it closes, when you switch devices and when the device drops. Kept in Liquid Control's settings: on the iPhone, the last five websites you opened on each computer; and your own shortcuts and text snippets. On the iPhone they're part of its backups, like other app settings, and your own shortcuts also sync to your other iPhones and iPads (see "Your other iPhones and iPads").
  • On your Mac, text from voice, Type, snippets and websites is pasted: Liquid Control puts it on the clipboard, presses ⌘V, and about a second later puts back what was there. For that second, other apps and Universal Clipboard can see it. If the clipboard was empty, the text stays on it; if another app copies in that second, its copy stays. Passwords and secrets never use the clipboard.
  • When you type with the iPhone's or watch's own keyboard or its dictation, that's Apple's keyboard, under Apple's privacy policy.

Passwords.

  • Sign in opens your Mac's own list of passkeys and passwords. macOS fills one in after Touch ID or your password; Liquid Control never sees it.
  • On iPhone, Type → Login takes a password from iOS AutoFill. Liquid Control holds it in memory only while it types it into the device you chose, then clears it. It is never stored, logged, pasted or sent anywhere else, and never sent to a TV.
    • A Bluetooth device: typed as key presses over the Bluetooth keyboard link. Never to a TV.
    • Your Mac (Liquid Control for Mac 1.6 or later): sent over the encrypted link and typed as key presses: never through the clipboard, never written to a log or a file.
    • A Liquid Control Stick: sent over the encrypted link and typed as USB key presses.

Code words (iPhone). Say a word of your own, like "pineapple", and Liquid Control types or presses what you set up for it, so people nearby hear only "pineapple".

  • Your code words (what you say, a label, and what each types or presses) are kept in Liquid Control's settings on your iPhone, are part of its backups, and sync to your other iPhones and iPads through your iCloud Keychain.
  • A secret (a password, a PIN) is typed once into a secure field and saved straight into the Keychain, as an iCloud Keychain item, so your other iPhones and iPads have it too (end-to-end encrypted; with iCloud Keychain off it stays on this iPhone). Liquid Control reads it only after Face ID or your passcode, each time it types it. It is typed as key presses, never pasted, shown or logged, and never sent to a TV. Deleting the code word deletes its secret.
  • Your speech is matched to your code words on the iPhone.
  • Understand other phrasings (off by default, iOS 26 and later) asks Apple's on-device model on your iPhone, never Apple's servers, which code word you meant. It sees your code words' labels and phrases and what you said, never what a code word types, and you confirm its pick with a tap.

Roku TV (iPhone)

  • To find your Roku, the iPhone asks iOS for Local Network access, then asks each address on your Wi-Fi network whether it's a Roku.
  • It keeps the Roku you pick: its address on your network, serial number, name and model.
  • It sends Roku's own remote commands to that Roku, over your local network only. They are plain HTTP without encryption, so anyone on the same network could see the keys and the text you send it. That's why Liquid Control never sends a password or a secret code word to a Roku.

Google TV and Android TV (iPhone)

  • To find your TV, the iPhone asks iOS for Local Network access, then looks for the TV's remote service on your Wi-Fi network (Bonjour).
  • To pair, you type the code the TV shows. Liquid Control makes a key and a certificate for you the first time (kept in your iCloud Keychain, so your other iPhones and iPads use the same), and the TV keeps that certificate.
  • It keeps each TV you pair: its name, its address on your network, and the fingerprint of the key it showed, which every later connection must show again.
  • It sends the TV's own remote keys and the text you type to it, over your local network only, encrypted with TLS. It never sends a password or a secret code word to a TV.

LG TVs (iPhone)

To find an LG TV, the iPhone asks iOS for Local Network access, then looks for it on your Wi-Fi network (its AirPlay name, and each address on your network for the TV's remote service), or uses the address you type. You allow Liquid Control on the TV's screen once. Liquid Control keeps the TV's name, its address on your network, the fingerprint of the key it showed, and the key the TV gave it, which is kept in your iCloud Keychain so your other iPhones and iPads can use the TV too. It sends the TV's remote buttons and the text you type to the TV only, over your local network, encrypted with TLS, and never anywhere else. It never sends a password or a secret code word to a TV.

Bluetooth devices (iPhone)

  • The iPhone shows up as "Liquid Control" to nearby devices, as a Bluetooth keyboard and mouse, only while Add a device is waiting for a Bluetooth device, while a Bluetooth device you added is in use, and while one you added stays connected when not in use (Settings → the device; on for computers and TVs, off for an iPad or phone). Otherwise it stops two minutes after you switch away.
  • A device has to pair with it before it can read your keys or pointer, and your input goes only to the device you chose in Liquid Control. A device that connects without being added gets nothing: Liquid Control asks whether to add it. Without pairing, a nearby device can read only fixed details about the keyboard and the iPhone's battery level, as with any Bluetooth keyboard.
  • Liquid Control keeps, for each device you add, the name and kind you gave it, what it runs, and its Bluetooth ID.
  • To unpair, forget Liquid Control (or your iPhone) on the other device.

Control Center, Siri and Shortcuts (iPhone)

  • Liquid Control's Control Center controls, Siri ("Switch Liquid Control to Living Room TV") and Shortcuts can open the remote on one of your devices. For that, Liquid Control keeps each device's name and kind where its own controls can read them, and gives them to iOS so Siri and Shortcuts can offer them. Never an address, serial number, key or anything you type.
  • A control or Shortcut you set up keeps the device's name and a code for it, made on your iPhone, that says nothing else about it. Shortcuts sync through your iCloud account if you have that turned on.
  • The controls, "Open Liquid Control remote" and "Switch Liquid Control to …" only open Liquid Control and choose the device. They never send input.

Motion

In Air mode the watch or iPhone reads its gyroscope and accelerometer to move the pointer and recognise pinches and flicks, and only while Air mode is the pointer and the app is open. Only the resulting pointer moves, clicks, scrolls and shortcuts go out. Motion data isn't stored.

Logs and Send Link Log

The link log on your Mac. Liquid Control for Mac keeps a log of its links to your watch and iPhone, so a dropped connection can be explained afterwards: ~/Library/Logs/Liquid Control/link.log, with the older part in link.1.log (about 1 MB at most in all). It's always on. It holds:

  • times; connections, drops, pairing and pausing; Bluetooth states; the apps' versions and build numbers; free memory; whether an app was in front;
  • short tags for the devices (4 characters of their Bluetooth or key IDs);
  • what the watch and iPhone noted since their last connection (below).

The watch's and iPhone's log. Each keeps its last 400 notes in Liquid Control's settings (on the iPhone, part of its backups). Each time it connects, and as they happen while it's connected, it sends the notes it hasn't sent yet to the device it connects to, a Stick included: your Mac adds them to its link log, and an iPhone that a watch connects through adds them to its own notes. The notes are the same kind of events, and also:

  • how long you held 🎤, timings, how many characters were typed, the name of a key a voice command pressed, and a code word's random ID;
  • whisper mode on or off, the kind of microphone (never its name), and the Whisper test's steps;
  • error messages from iOS and watchOS, such as Bluetooth's;
  • the model of a Roku you pick (for example "Roku Ultra");
  • the name of a device you switch to in Devices, such as your Mac's name;
  • on the iPhone, the Bluetooth keyboard's state: whether it's visible to other devices, how many are connected, and their short IDs.

The logs never hold what you type or say, a code word's phrase, label or value, a password, a pairing code or key, or audio. They stay on your devices unless you send them.

Send Link Log. In Liquid Control for Mac's menu, Send Link Log… asks whether to open a new Mail message to us with the log attached (link.log, and link.1.log when there's an older part), or to show the files in Finder for the email app you use. You see the message and the files first, and nothing is sent unless you press Send. If Mail can't take them, Finder shows them instead. An email you send gives us the log, your email address and what you write. We use a link log only to find and fix connection problems, and delete it when you ask.

Send diagnostics (iPhone). Settings → Help → Send diagnostics makes a text file of the iPhone's own notes and the state of its links (each device's kind and status, never its name), and opens the share sheet: you choose where it goes (AirDrop, Mail, Files) or cancel. Nothing is sent by Liquid Control itself. It holds the same kinds of notes as above, never what you typed or said, a password or a code word's value.

Record test log (Mac). A measuring tool, off by default, in the testing tools that Settings → Advanced can add to the menu. While it's on, it saves timings and counts in a file in ~/Library/Logs/Liquid Control/, with the macOS version and the key a voice command pressed, never what you typed or said. The file stays until you delete it.

From Apple. If you test Liquid Control with TestFlight, Apple gives us the crash reports and feedback you choose to send, and counts of sessions and crashes. Crash logs can also reach us from Apple when you've chosen to share analytics with app developers. That data comes from Apple, under Apple's privacy policy.

Other options on your Mac

These are off by default and under Settings → Advanced:

  • Keypad: Liquid Control watches key presses for the keys you assign to it (F13–F18 by default) and lets every other key through untouched.
  • Count how often I use the Mac's own trackpad: Liquid Control notes only that the Mac's own keyboard or pointer was used, as a count it keeps in memory (and in the test log, with the time, while that's on). Never which key or where. Turning it off takes effect when Liquid Control next opens.
  • AirPods: connects AirPods already paired with your Mac when your watch or iPhone connects.

The Liquid Control Stick

The Stick (hardware, not sold yet) takes the same encrypted input as Liquid Control for Mac and types it as a USB keyboard and mouse. It keeps its own key and its paired remotes' public keys, has no network connection, and keeps no input.

What we collect

Nothing automatically. If you email us (a link log, a question), we get what you send and use it only to answer you or fix the problem. Liquid Control has:

  • no accounts;
  • no analytics;
  • no advertising;
  • no tracking, and no third-party code that tracks you.

The watch app in the public beta and on the App Store has no HealthKit. A test build that keeps the watch awake runs a workout session: it asks to save workouts, and saves or reads nothing.
